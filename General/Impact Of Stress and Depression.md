Here’s the breakdown:
1. Chronic stress
Long-term stress keeps your body’s stress hormone cortisol elevated.
High cortisol can damage the hippocampus — the brain region critical for memory and learning, which is one of the first areas affected in Alzheimer’s.
Chronic stress is also linked to inflammation and reduced blood flow to the brain, both of which are risk factors for dementia.
2. Depression
Late-life depression (depression occurring in older age) is strongly associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s.
It may be a risk factor (contributing to changes in the brain that later lead to dementia) or an early symptom (since Alzheimer’s can initially affect mood and motivation before memory).
Depression can also reduce brain plasticity (the ability to form new neural connections), making the brain more vulnerable.
3. The stress–depression–Alzheimer’s link
Stress can worsen depression, and depression can make it harder to manage stress — creating a loop that can accelerate brain aging.
Both conditions can lead to lifestyle changes (poor sleep, inactivity, poor diet, social isolation) that increase Alzheimer’s risk.
4. Protective steps
While genetics and age are the biggest risk factors for Alzheimer’s, managing stress and depression can help protect brain health:
Stay mentally active (reading, puzzles, learning new skills)
Exercise regularly (especially aerobic activities)
Get quality sleep
Maintain social connections
Seek treatment for depression and anxiety early
